## 1. Burpee Broad Jump: 10:03 to 6:30–7:00

### 1.1 Rules (2025/26)
- Chest touches the floor on every rep. Hands may be at most 30 cm in front of the feet. On the stand-up, the feet may not pass the hand position [2].
- Take-off and landing must be two-footed with feet parallel. No shuffle steps forward between reps [2].
- Stepping up one leg at a time is legal. The 2025/26 rulebook clarifies you may use your knees to get up to standing [2][8].
- Penalties: after one warning, each further infringement costs a 15-second time penalty. Distance penalties were removed [8].
- Some older guides still quote a 5 m penalty [7]. Use the current rule.

### 1.2 Technique for a tall, heavy athlete
**Step down, step up [COACH, broad agreement]**
- Most top athletes step up one leg at a time over 80 m. Jumping both feet in is faster per rep but raises heart rate more [2].
- One coach says only elite athletes benefit from the two-foot jump-in. For most competitors, the energy cost outweighs the small time saving [6].
- Coming up onto one knee before stepping to the foot saves energy [2].
- On the way down: hands under the shoulders, then step or lightly hop the feet back under control. Go only as low as the standard requires, with elbows close to the body [4].
- For 97 kg [ANALYSIS]: a two-foot jump-back and jump-in lifts and catches 97 kg twice more per rep, about 80 extra loaded impacts over 80 m. Step down and step up.

**Jump horizontally, not up [COACH]**
- Chest slightly forward at take-off. Drive from the hips rather than the quads. Swing the arms forward, not upward. Cue: "push the floor back" [4][5].
- Common faults: landing too deep, excessive vertical lift, overusing the quads [5].
- Land softly with knees bent and feet under your centre of mass [2][4].
- Drive the arms hard. They add distance without extra leg fatigue [2].
- Minimise standing tall between reps. Many athletes keep a slight forward lean throughout [2].

**Use sub-maximal jumps [COACH]**
- One coach estimates 15 reps with small jumps take about the same time as 10 reps with maximum jumps, but you finish much fresher. Max jumps spike heart rate and wreck the next run [6].
- Consistent jumps beat a few huge ones followed by tiny ones [3][2].

### 1.3 Jump distance and rep count for 193 cm
- Rep count depends only on jump length. Most athletes need about 40–60 reps for 80 m [2]. One guide uses 1.5–2 m per jump as a planning anchor [24].
- Estimate for you [ANALYSIS, no source on height]: a comfortable, repeatable fatigued jump should be about 1.9–2.1 m, which is **38–42 reps**.
- Do not chase 2.4 m+. Your height is an advantage only if you stay sub-maximal.
- Measure it: one guide recommends a 10–20 m race-pace test to find your real distance per rep [7]. Do 20 m at race effort, count reps, and multiply by 4.

### 1.4 Pacing and cadence
**Correction to the brief [ANALYSIS]**
- One rep every 5–6 s would finish 80 m in about 3:20–4:00. That is near-elite (elite men averaged 2:21 in the 2022 data [23]; DataLab's 1:30 target is about 5:00 [21]).
- For a **6:45 target (405 s)** at 40 reps you need about **one rep every 10 s, roughly 6 reps per minute**. At 44 reps it is about 9.2 s per rep.

**Practical rhythm [COACH + ANALYSIS]**
- Move in about 5–6 s: drop, step back, chest down, step up, jump. Then take 2–3 breaths, 3–4 s, standing right after landing.
- Take any rest immediately after landing, not mid-movement [6][3].
- Roxlyfe's author rests lying on the floor because heart rate seems to drop faster. Test both [2].
- Split the 80 m into four 20 m blocks of about 10 reps and about 1:40 each [3][ANALYSIS].
- Don't sprint the first 20 m. Early over-jumping drains you by about 30 m [3][7].

**Breathing [COACH]**
- Exhale on the drop, inhale on the push-up, exhale forcefully on the jump. Never hold your breath through transitions [5][4].
- Roxlyfe adds: exhale on the jump, quick inhale on the way down [2].

### 1.5 Training progression (16 weeks)
Coach-sourced drills: technique ladders of 10/20/30 m; EMOM 8–10 reps; BBJ after sled work or 1 km runs; distance pyramids building to 80 m [2]. Also 5×20 m with 90 s rest, or 3×40 m with 2 min rest [3]. Also 3 rounds of 60 s max burpees, 60 s rest, then 800 m fast run [25].

My sequencing [ANALYSIS], built from those drills:

| Weeks | Main BBJ session (1×/wk) | Low-impact burpee capacity (1×/wk) |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | EMOM 10: 4 BBJ on mats or turf (40 jumps) | 5×20 step-back/step-up burpees, no jump, 60 s rest. Goal: 100 burpees in under 7:00 by week 4 |
| 5–8 | EMOM 12: 5 BBJ (60 jumps), or 5×20 m BBJ with 90 s rest | 3 rounds of 60 s burpees + 60 s rest + 3 min hard bike/row |
| 9–12 | 3×40 m BBJ with 2 min rest, after 1 km bike/row at race effort; or "sled pull 50 m → 1 km → 80 m BBJ" in half-sims | Burpee + 1 KB swing ×10 EMOM for hip power without landings |
| 13–14 | 80 m BBJ for time, fresh (week 13) and inside a full or half sim | Maintenance |
| 15–16 | 2×20 m BBJ at race rhythm only | None |

**Benchmarks to track [ANALYSIS]:** fresh 80 m under 5:45 by week 10 projects to about 6:30–6:45 in the race.

### 1.6 Shins and impact
- Broad-jump landings are the highest-impact part of the station [ANALYSIS]. Running shoes and the HYROX carpet over concrete add little damping; HYROX lays thin carpet over concrete [26].
- Recommendations [ANALYSIS / coach opinion, no direct study]:
  - Cap jump contacts per week. Start around 40 and add about 10–20 per week only if shins are calm the next morning.
  - Jump on turf or gym mats, not concrete.
  - Build most burpee fitness with no-jump burpees, burpee + KB swing, and bike/rower sprints.
  - Keep the step-down/step-up pattern in every session. It removes about 80 loaded impacts per 80 m.
- Calf and soleus strength matter. See §8.

## 2. Wall Balls: 9:04 to 5:30–6:00

### 2.1 Standards
- Open men: 6 kg ball, 3 m (10 ft) target, 100 reps [9][10].
- A valid rep needs a squat with **hips lower than knees**, a two-hand throw to the target zone, and a catch (or let it drop and reset) [9].
- If the ball hits the floor you must stand fully tall before the next squat. You can't catch a bounce [9].
- No warnings: each rep is valid or not. A squat-depth box may be added at the judge's discretion and can't be used to sit [9].
- PureGym describes depth as "knees below 90°" [10]. Roxlyfe's "hips below knees" matches the judging standard. Train to hip crease below the top of the knee [9].
- Gloves are allowed if worn or carried from start to finish. Gymnastics grips are not allowed. Your own powdered chalk at wall balls is a 2-minute penalty [9].
- Competition balls are made by Centr. Train with a similar grippy 6 kg ball [9].

### 2.2 Being tall: shorter throw, longer squat
**Shorter throw: confirmed by practitioners [COACH]**
- Shorter athletes have a longer ball path to the target and so a bigger energy cost [15].
- For a tall athlete you may not need every available push [16].
- Geometry [ANALYSIS]: standing reach is roughly 1.3× height, so yours is about 2.5 m versus about 2.25 m for a 175 cm athlete. You need about 0.5 m of ball flight above your hands rather than about 0.75 m. Throw only high enough to hit the target; extra height wastes energy and makes the catch less predictable [9].

**Longer squat: the cost [ANALYSIS]**
- Long femurs mean more range of motion to get the hip below the knee, so quads tire faster. That is why your quad endurance after lunges matters (see §5).
- Fixes:
  - Slightly wider stance with toes turned out [9][10].
  - Stand about an arm's length from the target [9][27].
  - Start the next squat as you catch rather than catching with straight legs and then descending. This uses the stretch-shortening cycle [9][12].
  - Hold the ball close to the chest, elbows in [10][9].
  - Drive with the legs; arms extend naturally [10][9].

**Breathing [COACH]:** inhale on the way down, exhale on the drive and release [9][10].

### 2.3 Set and rest strategy
**Coach and data positions**
- HyroxDataLab (via search summary): competitive athletes 4×25 or 5×20 with 3–5 s rest; recreational 5×20 with 5–8 s rest. For most athletes, planned sets are faster than trying to go unbroken [11].
- FitnessVolt: sets of 10–15 on a fixed schedule with 5–10 s standing rest, e.g. 15-10-10-10-10-10-10-10-5 [12].
- Prommer: descending 25-15-10-5 splits or 5×20, and warns against starting at a pace you can't hold [13].
- Roxlyfe: a descending 16-15-14-13-12-11-10-9 plan, and leaving 5–10 reps in reserve on the first set [9].
- PureGym example: 15-15-10-10 style sets with a steady cycle time [10].
- Cole Learn, quoted by Roxlyfe, on putting the ball down: "if I set the ball down, I'm toast" [9]. Rest with the ball held, not dropped.

**The math [ANALYSIS]**
- A rep takes about 2.3–2.5 s, so 100 reps is about 4:00 of pure work.
- 6:00 leaves about 2:00 for all rests. 9:04 means about 5 minutes of rest.

**Race plan by phase [ANALYSIS]**
- Weeks 1–8 target (about 6:00): 15-15-15-12-12-10-10-11. Seven breaks, each **at most 10 breaths or about 12 s**, ball held at the chest or hips.
- Weeks 9–16 target (about 5:30): 20-20-20-20-20, at most 10 s rest. Fall back to 10s only for the last 20 if needed.
- Rule: count the rest out loud. Never take an unplanned break; a forced break after failure costs 30–60 s.

### 2.4 Grip, forearms and quads [COACH + ANALYSIS]
- Forearm fatigue from sled pull and farmers carry shows up as a sloppy catch. Catch with soft palms and elbows under the ball [10]. Gloves may help on a slippery ball [9].
- Quad endurance [10]: front squats, thrusters, wall sits, high-rep air squats. Add tempo goblet squats (3-1-1), 3×20.

### 2.5 Training progression
Coach and data sources:
- EMOM 10 × 10 reps at race weight [9].
- "Gillingham": 90 s max reps, 25 s rest, × 5 rounds [9].
- 150 reps for time ("Karen") [9].
- Compromised work: 3–4 rounds of run + 20–25 wall balls, then the full 100 after a hard run or HYROX session nearer the race [9].
- Pyramid 30-20-10 with 60 s rest, plus a timed 100 for a benchmark [14].
- PureGym EMOM 10: odd minutes 15–20 wall balls, even minutes 10–15 cal SkiErg or bike, 1–2×/week at the end of sessions [10].
- An occasional heavier ball [13]. Jake Dearden uses 30 unbroken reps at 9 kg alongside 500 m runs [28].
- Hunter McIntyre named sleds, burpee form and being unbroken on wall balls as what makes the difference now [29].

My block layout [ANALYSIS]:

| Weeks | Session A (after a strength day) | Session B (in conditioning or sim) |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | EMOM 10 × 10 → EMOM 12 × 12 | 30-20-10 with 60 s rest; timed 100 in week 1 as a baseline |
| 5–8 | Gillingham 5 rounds (90 s / 25 s) | PureGym EMOM 10, wall balls alternating with BikeErg |
| 9–12 | "Karen" 150 for time in week 9 and week 12. Target under 8:30, then under 7:45 | 3–4 rounds: 4 min bike/row at threshold (or 1 km run if shins allow) + 25 wall balls |
| 13–14 | 25 m lunges + 1 km + **100 wall balls** for time (the race finish) | Full or half sim ending in wall balls |
| 15–16 | 3×20 at race rhythm | None |

**Benchmark [ANALYSIS]:** a fresh 100 in under 4:30 with a 20s plan generally transfers to about 5:30–6:00 at the end of the race.

## 3. Sleds

### 3.1 Specs
- Open men: push **152 kg** and pull **103 kg**, both including the sled. Each is 4 × 12.5 m = 50 m [19][17][18].
- The sled must fully cross the line before you change direction [17][18].
- Sled pull: stay inside the athlete box (Velites describes a 2 m work area). Don't step on the solid white line at the front of the box [17][8].
- After one warning, each infringement costs 15 s [8].

### 3.2 Sled push: 5:03 to about 4:00 [COACH]
- **Arm position:**
  - Arms-extended with a low, near-parallel body line is suggested for taller athletes or strong horizontal pushers [18].
  - The "hug" position (forearms on the poles, elbows tight) uses more posterior chain and suits shorter athletes [18].
  - Prommer describes three options: shoulder push with arms bent about 90°, full lockout, or arm-bar/body contact for heavy loads [26].
  - For you [ANALYSIS]: test long-arm lockout against bent-arm in training and pick one before race day [18].
- **Steps and force:** short, powerful steps pushing through the toes. Long strides lose traction [18]. Drop your hands so force goes forward, not down [18].
- **Starting from a stop:** starting the sled costs far more than keeping it moving [32]. Lean in, take explosive first steps, and don't let go or rest mid-push [18].
- Some coaches suggest breaking each length into two pieces with a mid-length pause rather than one long grind [6]. This conflicts with the "don't stop" advice above. If you stop, reset your posture fully, then restart explosively [18].
- **Training:**
  - 4–6 × 25–50 m at race weight or heavier [26].
  - 4 rounds of 400 m run + 25 m push + 1:30 rest [18].
  - Prommer sims: 1 km run → 50 m push → 1 km run (×2), and 500 m SkiErg → 25 m push (×4) [26].

### 3.3 Sled pull: 6:42 to about 5:00 [COACH]
**Techniques**
- Hand-over-hand from a wide, flat-footed squat. Arms stay long, and the lats and hamstrings drive the pull [17].
- Walk-back: an explosive hip drive, then step back while the sled is moving [31][17].
- Combined: walk-back for the early metres, then static hand-over-hand to finish [17]. Jake Dearden: hips back, lean back slightly, pull one arm at a time [28].

**For 193 cm [ANALYSIS]:** long arms give a long pull stroke, and long legs cover a lot of rope per back-step. A hip-driven walk-back for the first 8–10 m of each length is likely your best tool. Watch the back line of the box.

**Rope management**
- Keep the rope taut from the first pull. Slack wastes the first pull [17].
- Throw the rope to the side, never behind you, so you don't trip on it [17][31].
- For a short break, keep hold of the rope. Re-tensioning costs more than a 2 s pause [17].

**Grip and chalk**
- Event chalk is provided at the sled pull and farmers carry [9].
- Decide on gloves or chalk in advance and train with your choice [17].

**Training**
- Train heavier than race weight. Add a sandbag in front of the sled to mimic carpet [17].
- 4 rounds of 400 m run + 25 m pull + 40 double-unders. Reduce the load if grip fails before round 3 [17].
- Accessories: strict pull-ups, rows, dead hangs, heavy farmers carries [17].
- In the 2022 data, average men's pulls took about 78% longer than their pushes; elites' took about 18% longer. Yours is 33% longer: 6:42 vs 5:03 [23][ANALYSIS].

### 3.4 Carpet friction and venue variation [COACH; not quantified]
- HYROX lays thin carpet over concrete, which creates more friction than gym turf. Most coaches recommend training 10–15% heavier than race weight to match race effort [26].
- Surface (carpet vs turf) is a critical variable in the coefficient of friction [32]. Venues use carpet or staging platforms, both of which raise resistance [18].
- No source measured how friction differs between venues. Treat sled times as venue-dependent, and test your race shoes on a sled for grip [26].

## 4. Roxzone: 7:46 to 5:00–5:30

**Benchmarks [DATA]**
- Utrecht analysis (825 athletes, via search summary): men averaged 5:57 total, about 45 s per transition. Recreational athletes about 52 s per transition vs about 28 s for elites; sub-1:10 elites about 3:41 [30].
- Roxlyfe: elite men spend about 75% of their average 1 km split in the Roxzone; average men about 131%. Aim for 75–90% [23].
- You [ANALYSIS]: 7:46 against a 5:40–6:00 average run is about 130–135%, the same as "average." Reaching 90% of 5:40 is about 5:06.
- Roxzone length differs by venue. Compare your Rio Roxzone to the Rio field, not to other cities [30].

**Fixes [COACH]**
1. **Learn the floor plan.** The map usually arrives 2–3 days before the race. Memorise it, then walk the venue on arrival [33]. Know your lane and station setup [30]. Avoid wrong turns into stations [34].
2. **Treat it as running, not resting.** Use the cue "Roxzone floor is lava." Many athletes slow down on entry without noticing [33]. Keep moving, even slowly [30].
3. **Drink less often.** Pre-hydrate and take quick sips only; long drink stops are a hidden time killer [33][34]. You may not need to drink at every visit [35].
4. **Prepare gear in advance.** Gloves on or off before the station, not in the lane [ANALYSIS].
5. **Practise transitions.** Time them in training [36]. Use run-to-station bricks [37].
6. **Expect the first 30–60 s after a station to hurt.** Much of that happens inside the Roxzone. Practise jogging out of stations [33].

**Training drill [ANALYSIS]:** in every simulation, put the "station" 30–50 m from the run loop and jog in and out at easy-run pace (about 6:00–6:30/km), not a walk. A 16-transition race at 20 s faster each saves about 5:20.

## 5. Compromised running and the Run 8 collapse

**What the data say [DATA]**
- Run 8 shows the biggest fade on average: 1:53 slower than Run 1 across 12,479 results (DataLab, via search summary) [38].
- DataLab's Utrecht analysis (via search summary) found lunge performance showed essentially no link to Run 8 time. Run 8 was strongly correlated with wall-ball performance (r = 0.738) [39].
- My reading [ANALYSIS]: Run 8 collapse is mostly a late-race durability and fuelling problem shared with wall balls, not simply going too hard on lunges.
- Other sources say Run 5, after BBJ, is often the slowest [40]. Yours were Run 8 (8:19) and Run 4 (6:15, after the sleds).

**Likely causes in your case [ANALYSIS]**
1. Quad and glute fatigue from lunges, made worse by long femurs.
2. Low glycogen about 80+ min in, with no in-race carbohydrate (see §9).
3. Dread of wall balls makes you hold back.
4. Possibly walking. 8:19 is about 2:30 slower than your other runs.

**Pacing strategy [COACH/DATA]**
- Run Runs 1–2 slightly conservatively, hold target on 3–4, and accept a modest slowdown on 5–8. Pace by effort, not speed [38].
- Elite men hold fairly even splits; average men start too fast [23].
- Matt Fitzgerald frames HYROX as a sustained, threshold-like effort [41].
- Red Bull's coach: run a pace you could sustain for about 75 minutes, and only go all-out after the lunges [42].
- Recommendation [ANALYSIS]: **even effort, not negative splits.** Run 8 target is 6:00–6:15. The first 200 m is a deliberate shuffle-to-jog to let the quads recover; then settle.

**Training methods [COACH]**
- Strength-into-run sets: 20 lunges → 400 m, 15 heavy wall balls → 400 m [25].
- Station → run chains: 500 m SkiErg → 1 km → 30 m sled → 1 km → 500 m row → 1 km [25].
- Frequency: intermediate athletes 1–2 sessions/week [25].
- Always lunge after running to reproduce race fatigue [43].
- PureGym: 800 m runs + 20 m lunges + wall balls with 2 min rest [44].
- **Run 8 brick [ANALYSIS], weeks 9–14:** 2–3 × (50 m sandbag lunges 20 kg → 1 km at Run 8 target pace → 30 wall balls), 3 min rest between rounds.
- **While shins are limited:** replace the 1 km with 4 min BikeErg or rower at threshold. Brandt et al. explicitly suggest limiting running and substituting or combining it with rowing or skiing, while practising running after upper- and lower-body efforts [1].

## 6. Weekly structure, periodisation and taper (6–8 h/week)

### 6.1 Principles
- **Endurance first** [EVIDENCE, n=11]: VO2max and endurance volume were the strongest predictors of finish time [1].
- **Intensity split** [COACH]: about 80% of endurance work easy (Zone 1–2) and 20% hard. Spend about half of training time running when healthy [41].
- **Running volume vs injury** [PREPRINT]: higher HYROX-specific training frequency was the only factor independently associated with injury (adjusted OR 1.61) [2b].
- **Example plans** [COACH]:
  - A 5.5 h/week TrainingPeaks plan uses 2 gym + 4 run sessions in 3-build + 1-recovery cycles. In the race block, the long run is replaced with race simulations [45].
  - A 5–6.5 h plan uses 3 runs + 2 strength + 2 custom sessions [46].

### 6.2 Template week, about 7–7.5 h, shin-adjusted [ANALYSIS]

| Day | Session | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | **Strength A:** squat pattern, sled push, calves/soleus. Plus wall-ball EMOM finisher | 60–70 min |
| Tue | **Engine intervals, bike/row:** e.g. 4×8 min threshold (RPE 7) or 5×3 min VO2 (RPE 8–9) | 50–60 min |
| Wed | **Easy run** (pain-guided, 20–40 min) **or** Zone 2 bike 60 min. Plus 15 min prehab | 45–60 min |
| Thu | **Strength B:** hinge, pull, sled pull, grip. Plus BBJ or low-impact burpee block | 60–70 min |
| Fri | Off, or 30–40 min Zone 2 bike + mobility | 0–40 min |
| Sat | **HYROX conditioning / compromised session or simulation** | 75–100 min |
| Sun | **Long Zone 2:** bike or row 75–90 min, plus 10–20 min easy run if tolerated | 75–100 min |

- Separate strength and hard endurance by at least 6 h, ideally on different days. See §7 [47].
- Cross-training [EVIDENCE, older and small]:
  - One trial replaced 50% of running volume with cycling for 5 weeks and found no loss of VO2max or 5 km performance (Mutton et al. 1993, per secondary summary) [48].
  - A secondary source suggests cycling at a slightly higher perceived effort to match a run [48].
  - Keep some running-specific intensity once pain allows, because cycling does not train running economy [48].

### 6.3 16-week periodisation (30 Nov 2026 to mid-March 2027) [ANALYSIS, built on the 3:1 model [45] and taper evidence [49]]

| Block | Weeks | Focus | Simulations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base / rebuild** | 1–3, deload week 4 | Shin rehab and run reintroduction; bike/row Zone 2 + threshold; strength base; BBJ/wall-ball technique EMOMs; baseline tests in week 1: 100 wall balls, 20 m BBJ test, 5×12.5 m sled push/pull | None |
| **Build 1** | 5–7, deload week 8 | Threshold volume; station capacity (Gillingham, BBJ 20 m intervals, heavy sleds); run volume up only if pain-free | Half sim (stations 1–4 or 5–8 with 1 km legs) in weeks 6 and 7 |
| **Build 2 / specific** | 9–11, lighter week 12 | Compromised bricks; race-pace work; Karen tests; Run 8 bricks | Half sim week 9; **full sim week 11** (or 2 half-sims split across the weekend) |
| **Peak** | 13–14 | Race-pace sharpening; strength maintenance (1–2 sets, heavy, low reps) | **Last hard sim in week 13, 14–18 days out.** Week 14: short race-pace pieces only |
| **Taper** | 15–16 | Cut volume 41–60% progressively over about 2 weeks; keep intensity and frequency [49] | None. Hardest session at least 7 days before the race [50] |

**Taper specifics**
- Evidence: Bosquet et al.'s meta-analysis found the best strategy was about a 2-week taper with volume reduced exponentially by 41–60% and no change to intensity or frequency [EVIDENCE, 49].
- Coach advice: 7–14 days; start with about a 10–15% cut and build toward about 50%; keep running speeds and lifting weights unchanged [51].
- Last training session about 2 days out; rest or a walk the day before [51].
- Some coaches say the hardest session or simulation should be at least a week before the race [50].

**Simulation frequency [COACH/ANALYSIS]**
- Sources do not agree on a number [50].
- One first-timer recommends a full or partial simulation 3–4 weeks out [52].
- For you: half-sims every 2–3 weeks from week 6, and 1–2 full sims total (weeks 11 and 13).

## 7. Strength targets and a minimal plan

**Benchmarks [COACH, not official]**
- BoxRox suggests back squat about bodyweight × 5 and deadlift 1–1.25× bodyweight as Open readiness [53].
- Elite 15 men had median 3RMs of about 132 kg squat and 165 kg deadlift (older data) [54].

**My targets for you at 97 kg [ANALYSIS]**
- Back or front squat: 100 kg × 5.
- Trap-bar deadlift: about 140–150 kg × 5.
- Sled push: 4 × 12.5 m at about 110–115% of race load on your gym surface.
- Sled pull: 4 × 12.5 m at race load plus a sandbag, at about 5:00 pace.
- Pull-ups: 10 strict.
- Single-leg calf raise: 25+ reps per leg (see §8).

**Interference effect [EVIDENCE]**
- Schumann et al. (2022, 43 studies): concurrent training does not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength. Explosive strength was attenuated when both were done in the same session, but not when sessions were at least 3 h apart [47].
- Robineau et al.: 0 h and, to a lesser extent, 6 h gaps were less effective than 24 h. Avoid scheduling the two within 6 h [55].
- Strength training improves running economy by about 2–8% vs controls. Adding 2–3 strength sessions per week likely helps distance runners [56].

**Minimal-effective plan (2×/week, about 45–60 min) [ANALYSIS]**

| Strength A (Mon) | Strength B (Thu) |
|---|---|
| Front or back squat 3×5 → 4×4 (RPE 7–8) | Trap-bar or RDL 3×5 |
| Bulgarian split squat 3×8/leg (lunge durability) | Sled pull heavy 4×12.5 m + strict pull-ups 4×max−2 |
| Heavy sled push 4×12.5 m | Single-arm DB row 3×10 + farmers carry 3×50 m heavy |
| Soleus (bent-knee) calf raise 3×15–20 + tibialis raises 2×20 | Straight-knee calf raise 3×12 slow + Copenhagen plank 2×20 s |
| Finisher: wall-ball EMOM (see §2.5) | Finisher: BBJ or low-impact burpee block (see §1.5) |

- Weeks 13–16: drop to 2 sets and keep the loads.

## 8. Injury prevention

**Epidemiology [PREPRINT]**
- 418 HYROX athletes: 49.8% reported an injury in the past 12 months, mostly gradual-onset and lower-limb [2b].
- Knee was the most common site (20.8%). Tendon complaints were the most common type (41.6%) [2b].
- Higher HYROX-specific training frequency was independently associated with injury [2b].
- In the only lab study, the authors advise limiting running volume because of injury risk [1].

**Your shin and soleus pain**
- Treatment evidence for medial tibial stress syndrome ("shin splints") is weak. No treatment has been clearly shown to be best [EVIDENCE, 57].
- Return-to-run guidance for tibial bone stress injury is individualised; no validated criteria exist [EVIDENCE, 57b].
- Calf endurance: runners with shin splints managed about 23 single-leg calf raises vs about 33 in healthy controls (Madeley 2007, per secondary source) [58].
- A fatigued calf passes more load to the tibia. Build calf capacity, then restart running at the distance and pace you can tolerate before symptoms begin [58].
- **See a sports physio or doctor if you have** pinpoint bone tenderness, pain at rest or at night, or pain on single-leg hopping. These are standard red flags for a bone stress injury [ANALYSIS: standard clinical caution, not from a cited source here].

**Run reintroduction [ANALYSIS: common clinical practice]**
- Run/walk on soft or even surfaces, 2–3×/week.
- Progress only if pain during the run is at most 3/10 and has settled by the next morning.
- Keep high-intensity work on bike/rower until you have 3 weeks of pain-free easy running.

**Prehab, 15 min 3×/week [ANALYSIS]**
- Bent-knee soleus raises and straight-knee calf raises. Aim for 25+ single-leg reps.
- Tibialis raises.
- Copenhagen plank.
- Single-leg RDL.
- Spanish squat or wall sit (knees, wall-ball quads).
- Hip abduction.
- Thoracic and shoulder mobility for wall balls and SkiErg.

**Footwear [COACH]**
- Prioritise grip on the carpet: full rubber outsole coverage. Test on a sled before race day [26][59].
- A podiatrist-led ranking suggests most racers do better in a stable shoe balancing cushioning, grip and control than in aggressive carbon racers [60]. Carbon racers can feel unstable on lunges, sleds and farmers late in the race [60].
- For 97 kg with shin issues [ANALYSIS]: a stable, moderately cushioned daily trainer for most running, and a stable hybrid or HYROX shoe with good rubber for the race. Avoid soft high-stack carbon racers.

## 9. Race day

### 9.1 Warm-up, 20–30 min, ending 5–10 min before your wave [COACH]
1. 8–12 min easy aerobic work (bike, row or jog) at about 60–70% of max HR [61].
2. Dynamic mobility for ankles, hips, thoracic spine and shoulders. No long static stretching [61].
3. Activation: glute bridges, dead bugs, scapular work, light pogos or calf raises [61].
4. Rehearse at 50–60% effort: strides, light wall balls, bodyweight lunges [61].
5. Priming: 2–3 accelerations of 5–10 s, a few crisp wall balls [61].
- Centr suggests keeping machine work minimal, e.g. 3–5 easy min on the rower, one 10 m sled push, one 500 m SkiErg at goal pace [62].
- One coach suggests entering the warm-up zone 30–45 min before the start, beginning with 10 min easy bike [35].

### 9.2 Gear
- **Gloves:** allowed if worn or carried from start to finish; no gymnastics grips [9].
- **Chalk:** event chalk only, at sled pull and farmers carry. Your own chalk at wall balls is a 2-minute penalty [9].
- Use gloves you have trained in; never debut gloves on race day [63].
- **Shoes:** must be worn all race except at wall balls [8].
- **Knee sleeves:** I found no source on their rules or value for HYROX. Check the official rulebook at hyrox.com/rulebook [8]. They are optional; test in lunge sessions if you use them [ANALYSIS].
- **Spitting or clearing your nose on the carpet:** 2-minute penalty [8].

### 9.3 Nutrition (97 kg)
**Caffeine [EVIDENCE]**
- 3–6 mg/kg improves performance. About 60 min before is the most common timing [64].
- For you: **about 290 mg (3 mg/kg)** 45–60 min before the start, or a caffeine gel 30 min before [64][65].
- Trial it in a simulation first [64].

**Carbohydrate [COACH/sport-nutrition]**
- Day before: at least 8 g/kg (≈780 g) per Precision Hydration [65]. Other guides suggest about 6 g/kg over the two prior days, so 6–8 g/kg (≈580–780 g/day) is reasonable [66].
- 2–3 h before: high-carb, low-fibre meal [65].
- 1–2 h before: carb snack [65].
- About 30 min before: a gel or banana [65].

**In-race**
- For races of 75 min to 2 h, aim for 30–60 g carbs/hour from gels or chews, taken in easier moments [65].
- Your plan [ANALYSIS]:
  - Gel 1 (≈25–30 g) on Run 5, right after BBJ, when heart rate settles.
  - Gel 2 or a sip of a carb drink on Run 6 or 7, so carbs are in your system before lunges, Run 8 and wall balls. This targets the Run 8 collapse.
  - Practise exactly this in weeks 9–14 simulations.
- **Fluids and sodium:** pre-load sodium the night before and about 90 min pre-race; take quick sips in the Roxzone [65].
