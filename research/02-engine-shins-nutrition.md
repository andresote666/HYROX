# Research report: building VO2max mostly on bike and rower, MTSS-safe return to running, and fat loss for a sub-1:30 HYROX Open

**Athlete:** man, 35, 193 cm, 97 kg aiming for 94 kg, HRmax about 193 bpm. Coming back from medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS) and soleus pain. Current HYROX Open time about 1:43, goal under 1:30, race about 16 weeks away (mid-March 2027).
**Report date:** 2026-11-25. Citations are in square brackets and refer to the numbered sources at the end.

**Evidence labels used below:**
- **[STRONG]**: meta-analyses, RCTs or consensus statements.
- **[MODERATE]**: small RCTs or cohort studies.
- **[WEAK]**: case-control studies, models, expert opinion or secondary summaries.
- **(calc)**: my own arithmetic from cited numbers, not a study result.
- **(heuristic)**: a practical coaching rule I could not trace to a peer-reviewed source.

## 0. Context: what HYROX demands

- **Brandt et al. 2025 (n = 11 recreational athletes, simulated Open race):**
  - Total time was about 86.5 min: about 51 min running and about 33 min at stations.
  - About 80% of race time was spent at "very hard" heart rate (≥90% HRmax).
  - Lactate peaked around 8.5 mmol/L at the stations. The highest heart rate, lactate and effort ratings came at wall balls.
  - Faster finish times correlated with higher VO2max (p = 0.01), more endurance training volume (p = 0.04) and lower body fat % (p = 0.03).
  - The authors describe HYROX as endurance-dominant, with moderate-to-low maximal-strength demands [15]. [WEAK–MODERATE: small sample, correlations only.]
- **Implication:**
  - Going from 1:43 to under 1:30 means about 13% faster (calc).
  - The main levers are aerobic capacity (VO2max and threshold), running durability over 8 km, and station efficiency.
  - Losing 3 kg directly helps the weight-bearing parts (running, sled, burpee broad jumps).

## A. Developing VO2max

### A1. Key protocols and their evidence

| Protocol | Study / evidence | Dose | Result |

| **15 s on / 15 s off at 90–95% HRmax** (about 47 reps in the original) | Helgerud 2007 [1] | 3×/wk | +5.5% VO2max [MODERATE] |
| **4×8 min at about 90% HRmax, 2 min recovery** | Seiler 2013, SJMSS, recreational cyclists [9] | 2×/wk plus 2–3 easy sessions, 7 wk | 4×8 (about 90% HRpeak, about 9.6 mmol/L lactate) improved VO2peak, peak power and power at 4 mmol/L more than 4×4 (about 94% HRpeak, about 13.2 mmol/L) or 4×16 (about 88% HRpeak). **Pooled effects:**
- **Milanović 2015 meta-analysis (28 studies, ages 18–45):** both HIIT and continuous training produce large VO2max gains. HIIT adds a small extra benefit of about +1.2 mL/kg/min, and longer repetitions about +2.2 mL/kg/min [6]. [STRONG]
- **Bacon 2013 meta-analysis (37 studies, 334 subjects):** mean VO2max gain with interval-based training was **+0.51 L/min** (95% CI 0.43–0.60). Studies with longer intervals showed about **+0.8–0.9 L/min** [7]. [STRONG, but participants were mostly untrained or moderately trained]

### A2. Time spent above 90% of VO2max

- **Concept (Buchheit & Laursen 2013):**
  - HIIT sessions can be described by how long they keep you above 90% of VO2max.
  - The variables you can adjust are work intensity, work duration, recovery intensity and duration, number of reps and sets, and exercise mode [4,5].
  - Long intervals (2–5 min) need about ≥90–95% of the speed or power that elicits VO2max to accumulate this time.
  - Short intervals (15–30 s) need about 100–120% of that speed or power, with short and fairly active recoveries.
- **Dose:**
  - The optimal amount of time above 90% per session is **not established**.
  - Accumulating roughly **5–10 min above 90% VO2max per session** is a common practical target (heuristic based on [4]).
  - A 4×4 typically gives about 2–3 min above 90% per rep once the lag is overcome. A 5×5 or 6×3 gives more.
- **Heart-rate lag:**
  - Oxygen uptake and heart rate take about 1–2 min to rise at the start of a work bout [4].
  - So **heart rate is useless for steering 15–30 s intervals, and only valid in the last 2–3 min of 4-min reps.**
  - Prescribe by **power, pace and effort rating**. Use heart rate only to confirm afterwards (for example, HR at the end of reps 2–4 should reach ≥90% HRmax = **≥174 bpm**).
  - Heart rate across a 30/15 set rises steadily; a final HR at about 90%+ HRmax by rep 10–13 confirms the dose.

### A3. How often to do HIIT, and intensity distribution

- **Frequency:**
  - 2×/wk (Seiler [9]) to 3×/wk (Helgerud [1]; Rønnestad short blocks [2]) is the evidence-based range.
  - Recommendation: **2 sessions/wk as the base**, with occasional 3-session weeks during build blocks, **counting race-specific HYROX simulations as one of the hard sessions** (heuristic).
- **Polarized vs pyramidal distribution:**
  - Polarized means mostly easy work plus a little very hard work, with little in between. Pyramidal means mostly easy, a moderate amount at threshold, and the least at very hard.
  - Rosenblat 2025 (individual-participant network meta-analysis, 13 studies, 348 athletes): **no overall difference** between polarized and pyramidal for VO2max or time-trial performance. **Recreational athletes may respond better to pyramidal**, competitive athletes to polarized [10]. [STRONG–MODERATE]
  - Oliveira 2024 meta-analysis: polarized was superior for VO2peak, mostly in interventions under 12 wk, and not for other performance measures [11].
  - Rosenblat 2019: polarized beat threshold-focused training for time trials (3 studies) [12].
  - **Recommendation:** pyramidal for this recreational athlete.
    - About 75–80% of training time easy (below about 78% HRmax, ≤150 bpm).
    - About 10–15% at threshold (about 85–90% HRmax, 164–174 bpm). HYROX race-pace compromised work lives here.
    - About 5–10% at VO2max intensity.

### A4. Expected VO2max gain over 12–16 weeks

- A realistic range for a recreationally trained man with no prior structured HIIT is about **+4–8% in absolute VO2max**:
  - Helgerud 7.2% in 8 wk [1].
  - Bacon +0.51 L/min, which is about +5.3 mL/kg/min at 97 kg (calc) [7].
  - Wen: largest gains with 4–12-wk long-interval programmes [8].
- Gains are smaller if he already does regular HIIT. Individual response varies widely (Bacon found high heterogeneity, I² = 70) [7].
- **Weight loss adds to relative VO2max:** dropping 97 → 94 kg raises mL/kg/min by about **3.2% (calc)** at the same absolute VO2max.
- Combined, about +7–11% in relative VO2max is a plausible but optimistic target (calc).

### A5. Prescribing on the bike, rower and SkiErg

**Do these tests first (week 1), then retest in weeks 8 and 15:**
- **Bike:** a ramp test to exhaustion (peak aerobic power, roughly MAP) **or** a 5-min all-out effort.
- **Rower:** a 2000 m time trial, or a 5-min all-out effort if a 2k is too much early on.
- **SkiErg:** a 5-min all-out effort.

**Bike prescriptions:**

| Session | Intensity | Cadence | Heart-rate check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4×4 (3 min easy between) | About 90–95% of 5-min max power, or about 85–90% of ramp-test peak; effort about 8–9/10. Roughly 106–120% FTP in coaching terms (heuristic). | 85–100 rpm (heuristic; no strong evidence on optimal cadence for VO2max work) | ≥174 bpm (90%) by minutes 3–4 of reps 2–4 |
| 4×8 (2 min between) [9] | About 85–90% of 5-min power; effort 7–8/10 | 85–95 rpm | About 170–176 bpm (88–91%) late in reps |
| 3 × 13 × 30/15 (3 min between sets) [2,3] | 30 s at about 100–110% of ramp-test peak (the hardest power you can repeat 13 times); 15 s at about 50% | 90–105 rpm | Rising to ≥90% HRmax by the end of each set |

**Rower prescriptions** (Concept2: watts = 2.80 / (seconds per metre)³ [16]):
- 4×4 at about **2k pace + 3–6 s/500 m**. Example: a 1:45 2k split gives 1:48–1:51, which is about 85–92% of 2k power (calc) [16].
- 30/15 at about **2k pace to 2k pace − 2 s**.
- Stroke rate about 26–30 for long reps and 30–34 for short reps (heuristic).
- A rowing study supports intervals at about 95% of 2k power (2.5-min reps) and concluded that the **dose of HIIT matters more than the exact method** [17].
- **SkiErg:** use the same split logic relative to the 5-min test pace.

**Heart rate by mode:** max heart rate on the bike and rower is often a few beats lower than running (common observation, not verified here). If you never reach 174 bpm on the bike despite maximal power, use **mode-specific peak HR** from the test.

## B. How well bike and rower fitness carries over to running

**What the evidence shows:**
- **Tanaka 1994 review:** some VO2max gain transfers between modes, but cross-training never exceeds sport-specific training. Specificity matters more in highly trained athletes, and swimming transfers least [18]. [MODERATE, older]
- **Mutton 1993 (5 wk, 85–90% HRmax, run-only vs cycle+run, n = 11, VO2max about 55):** both groups improved treadmill VO2max, 5000 m and 1609 m times, with **no difference** between groups [19]. [WEAK: tiny n]
- **Flynn 1998 (20 well-trained runners, added cycling vs added running for 6 wk):** both groups improved 5 km similarly; cycling gave no extra benefit over more running [20]. [MODERATE]
- **Foster 1995 RCT:** maintaining run performance while replacing some running with cycling was possible when intensity was matched [21]. [MODERATE; details from abstract and secondary summary]
- **Etxebarria 2014 (14 triathletes, 2×/wk for 3 wk):** high-intensity **cycle** intervals improved a run-off-the-bike 5 km. **Longer 5-min bike intervals were more likely to benefit the 5 km run** than short sprints [22]. [MODERATE]
- **Paquette 2018 (high-school runners, 4 wk, 2 easy runs/wk replaced by cycling, elliptical or ElliptiGO):** outdoor cycling and ElliptiGO looked most effective [23]. [WEAK–MODERATE]
- **Running economy is more specific than VO2max:**
  - Running economy improves with running-specific training [25].
  - Pizza 1995 reported cycling cross-training was detrimental to running economy, though its reported outcomes are internally inconsistent [24]. [WEAK]
- **Deep-water running:**
  - Maintains VO2max, lactate threshold and running performance for up to about 6 wk in trained runners (Wilber 1996; Reilly 2003 review) [26,27].
  - Max heart rate and VO2 are lower in water [26]. [MODERATE]
- **Rowing, SkiErg and elliptical for running performance:** I found **no direct trials**. Assume they transfer central (heart, blood) adaptations like cycling does, but not leg-specific stiffness or running economy. [Evidence gap]
- **Maintenance dose (Spiering 2021 review):** endurance was maintained for up to 15 wk with as few as **2 sessions/wk** or **33–66% less volume, as long as intensity was kept** [28]. [MODERATE; general populations]

**Bottom line:**
- Bike and rower HIIT can carry most of the VO2max development (central adaptations) [1,7,19,22].
- Running is still needed for (a) running economy and 1 km pace feel [25], (b) bone, tendon and soleus tolerance to about 8 km of running under fatigue, and (c) run-to-station transitions.
- The minimum running dose needed to maintain economy and tissue tolerance **has not been established in trials** [Evidence gap]. The plan below is expert-opinion based.

**Recommended weekly running dose while returning from MTSS** (expert opinion, built on [39–41,76,77]):

| Phase (weeks from now) | Running | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| **R0** (until 5 consecutive days with no pain in daily life; hop ×10 pain-free) | 0 running. Bike, row and ski for all aerobic work. | Warden criteria for starting return to run [39] |
| **R1** (about wk 1–3) | Walk-run on alternate days, 3×/wk. Start with 5 × (1 min run / 1 min walk), about 10–15 min total, adding about 1–2 run minutes per session if green. Treadmill or even soft surface. | Progress **volume before speed** [39,40]. If symptoms are provoked, stop, rest a day and drop back a stage [41]. |

## C. MTSS and soleus: diagnosis, management and a daily self-check

### C1. What MTSS is

- Exercise-induced pain along the **posteromedial border of the distal two-thirds of the tibia**, with tenderness on palpation over a **diffuse area (>5 cm)** [44].
- Clinicians can diagnose it reliably from history and examination alone (inter-rater kappa 0.89) [33].
- **Mechanism:**
  - Histology does not support traction-driven periostitis.
  - Current understanding is a **bone stress reaction in which bone resorption outpaces formation in the tibial cortex**. It sits at the early end of a continuum: MTSS → stress reaction → stress fracture [34,78,79].
  - A crural fascia or traction contribution from the soleus and deep calf muscles is still debated [34].
- **Plantarflexor deficits:** athletes with MTSS do fewer single-leg heel raises (about **23 vs 33** in matched controls) [36]. Cause versus effect is unknown. [WEAK, case-control]

### C2. Risk factors relevant to this athlete

- **Meta-analyses:**
  - **Higher BMI** (SMD 0.24; Hamstra-Wright weighted mean difference 0.79 kg/m²).
  - **Greater navicular drop** (SMD 0.26; >10 mm risk ratio about 2.0).
  - **Prior MTSS**.
  - **Greater hip external-rotation range of motion (in males)**.
  - Less running experience.
  - Orthotic use (probably a marker of existing problems).
  - Female sex, and in Hamstra-Wright greater ankle plantarflexion range of motion [29,30,31]. [MODERATE]
- **Training errors:**
  - Weekly distance increases above 30% raise distance-related injury risk [76].
  - A single run more than 10% longer than the longest run in the past 30 days raises overuse injury rate (hazard ratio 1.64 for spikes of >10–30%; 2.28 for spikes >100%) [77]. [MODERATE, large cohort]
- **His profile:** BMI is about 26.0 (97 / 1.93²; calc). He has prior MTSS and a recent sudden volume increase. That is three established risk factors.

### C3. Telling MTSS apart from a tibial bone stress injury (needs a doctor and imaging)

| Feature | More like MTSS | Red flag for bone stress injury or fracture |
|---|---|---|
| Tenderness | Diffuse, >5 cm along the posteromedial border [44] | **Focal, point tenderness (<5 cm)**: treat as a stress fracture until ruled out [42,44]. Bony tenderness on palpation is the most significant exam finding [42]. |
| Single-leg hop | Can hop ≥10 times, perhaps with mild pain [43] | **Cannot hop 10 times, or sharp localised pain** [43] (simplified clinical sign, not a validated accuracy estimate) |
| Pain pattern | Early in a run, may ease during it, settles with rest | Pain when walking or at rest, **night pain** (low-quality evidence), worsening with each run, swelling |
| Imaging | MRI may show periosteal oedema (Fredericson grade 1) | Marrow oedema or fracture line (grades 2–4); MRI is the reference test [42,43] |

### C4. Evidence-based management

- **Treatment trials are weak:**
  - Winters 2013 systematic review (11 trials, all at high risk of bias): **no treatment proven effective**. Stretching and strengthening, compression stockings, braces, laser and pulsed electromagnetic fields were all unproven [32].
  - Moen 2012 RCT (n = 74): a **graded running programme** alone, plus calf stretching and strengthening, or plus compression stocking. **No difference**; full recovery took about **100–118 days** [35].
  - Expect weeks to months, not days.
- **Load management (core):**
  - A graded return to running that starts with walk-run.
  - Progress volume before speed and intensity, guided by symptoms [39,40,41].
  - Keep fitness with low-impact cross-training [26,27].
- **Cadence:**
  - Raising step rate 5–10% at the same speed reduces knee energy absorption, hip energy absorption at +10%, braking impulse and vertical centre-of-mass movement [45].
  - In a probabilistic model, a 10% shorter stride lowered tibial stress-fracture probability by about 3–6%, while more mileage raised it [46].
  - This is lab and model evidence, not injury-trial evidence. [MODERATE for mechanics, WEAK for injury prevention]
  - Practical: measure current easy-pace cadence (for example 160 spm) and aim for **+5% (about 168)**, then up to +10% (about 176) if comfortable. A tall runner does not need to chase 180.
- **Footwear and insoles:**
  - Cochrane (mostly military recruits): the evidence base is very weak. Custom insoles may reduce shin splints (one trial, n = 146). Fitting shoes to foot shape did not help [52].
  - Practical: use a comfortable, cushioned shoe you are used to, rotated as you like. [WEAK]
- **Calf and soleus strengthening:**
  - Not proven as a standalone treatment [32,35].
  - Still a sensible capacity goal given the endurance deficits in MTSS [36] and the plantarflexors' dominant role in vertical support during running [Dorn 2012; Hamner 2013, via search]. [WEAK]
- **Pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel/Thomeé):**
  - Developed for tendinopathy, not bone.
  - Pain during activity may reach **≤5/10**. Pain after activity is allowed but **must settle by the next morning**. Pain and stiffness must **not increase week to week** [38].
  - **For bone-related tibial pain, use a stricter during-run limit of ≤3/10 and stop if pain rises during the run.** This is expert opinion, in line with Warden's "stop if symptoms provoked" rule for bone stress [39,41].

**Strength targets:**
- **Single-leg heel raise to fatigue:**
  - Norm: median about **24 reps (men)** on a 10° incline [37]; a 2025 study reports about 25 [37].
  - MTSS athletes about 23 vs controls about 33 [36].
  - **Target ≥25–30 reps each side, with less than 10% difference between sides** (target is heuristic).
  - Do this test **once a week**, not daily, because it is fatiguing.
- **Seated soleus raise (knee about 90°):**
  - The commonly quoted "1.5–2× bodyweight" benchmark (about 145–195 kg external load for 8–12 reps, or isometric force) **has no peer-reviewed normative data I could verify**. Treat it as a long-term clinical goal, not a pass/fail criterion. [WEAK]
  - Programme: 2–3×/wk, 3–4 sets of 6–12 reps, slow tempo, building load about 5–10% per week if symptoms stay green.
- **Also:** straight-knee heel raises, tibialis posterior work, and hip and glute strength (hip external-rotation range of motion is a risk factor) [29–31]. [WEAK]

### C5. Daily self-check and traffic-light rules (for app logic)

**Morning tests (about 3 min, both legs, before training):**
1. **Morning pain** (getting up and walking): 0–10.
2. **Tibial palpation**: press firmly along the inner (posteromedial) border of the lower two-thirds of the shin. Record **highest pain 0–10** and whether tenderness is **diffuse (>5 cm) or a focal spot (<5 cm)** [44].
3. **Single-leg hop ×10** in place, each leg: pain 0–10, and whether all 10 were completed [43].
4. **Single-leg heel raise ×10** (a check, not to fatigue): pain 0–10.
5. **Yesterday's run**: highest pain during the run (0–10), and whether it had settled to baseline by this morning (yes/no) [38].

**Weekly checks (same day each week):**
- Heel raise to fatigue, counting reps and the difference between sides [36,37].
- Seated soleus load used.
- Optional: morning resting heart rate and HRV trend (section F).

**Traffic-light rules** (highest flag wins; thresholds are expert opinion built on [38,39,41,42,44]):

| Status | Criteria (any one triggers) | Action today |
|---|---|---|
| **GREEN** | Morning pain 0–1; palpation ≤2 and diffuse; hop ×10 done with pain ≤2; heel-raise pain ≤1; yesterday's run pain ≤3 and settled by morning | **Run as planned.** Progression allowed (≤10% longer than the longest run in 30 days [77]; weekly running volume +≤10–20%). |
| **YELLOW** | Morning pain 2–3; palpation 3–4 (diffuse); hop pain 3–4; yesterday's run pain 4–5, or not fully settled by morning; or 2 days in a row of rising scores | **No progression.** Replace the run with bike, rower or SkiErg at the same intensity and duration, or cut the run by 30–50% on a treadmill or soft surface at +5–10% cadence. Re-check tomorrow. **2 yellows in a row → no running for 2–3 days** (bike and rower OK). Skip burpee broad jumps and other jumping. |
| **RED** | Morning pain ≥4; palpation ≥5 **or any focal spot tenderness**; cannot complete 10 hops or hop pain ≥5; pain while walking, at rest or **at night**; swelling; limping; run pain went above 5 or got worse during the run | **No running or impact.** Only pain-free non-impact work (bike, rower, upper-body SkiErg); stop anything that provokes tibial pain. **Contact a sports medicine doctor** if red lasts >3 days, or **immediately** for focal tenderness, night or rest pain, or a failed hop test. |

**After a red episode:**
- Return to running only after **5 consecutive green days with no pain in daily life** [39].
- Restart **one stage lower** in the walk-run progression [41].

**When to see a doctor or physio:**
- Any red flag from C3 (focal tenderness, night or rest pain, cannot hop, swelling).
- No improvement after 2–3 weeks of reduced load.
- Recurrent yellow or red days despite following the plan.
- Numbness, tingling, or tight "bursting" calf pain that stops when exercise stops (possible exertional compartment syndrome; expert opinion).
- Signs of low energy availability (section E).
- An MRI is the appropriate imaging if bone stress injury is suspected [42].

## D. Running mechanics for a heavy, tall runner

- **Body mass and cost:**
  - Supporting body weight accounts for most of the metabolic cost of running (up to about 74% of the net cost).
  - Net metabolic rate scales roughly in proportion to body weight, and slightly more than proportionally when load is added [47]. [MODERATE]
  - Losing 3 kg (−3.1%) should cut absolute O2 cost at a given pace by about 2–3% (calc).
- **Body mass and impact:**
  - Ground reaction forces scale with body weight. Most studies report them per kg, which hides the absolute load on bone and tendon [51].
  - In absolute terms, 3 kg less is about 3% less force per step, accumulated over thousands of steps in an 8 km race (calc).
  - **Loading rate** (not peak force) was higher in runners with a history of stress fracture [50,51]. [MODERATE]
- **Cadence:** +5–10% step rate reduces braking, vertical oscillation and joint energy absorption [45]. A shorter stride lowers modelled tibial fracture probability [46]. A reasonable cue for a 193 cm, 97 kg runner, especially in HYROX's fatigued 1 km reps.
- **Surfaces:**
  - Treadmill and overground running are biomechanically largely comparable [48].
  - A 2022 meta-analysis (Keast et al.) found **lower tibial loading on the treadmill than overground (SMD −0.83)** [49]. This comes from the abstract and summary only; I could not access the full text.
  - Evidence on track versus road compliance and injury is mixed and confounded (BMI and other factors) [49].
  - Practical: do the early return-to-run stages (R1–R2) on a treadmill (about 1% incline), then add the race surface in R3–R4. HYROX venues are indoor; race-surface details were not verified.

## E. Fat loss while training for performance

### E1. Rate and size of the deficit

- **Rate:**
  - Garthe 2011 RCT (elite athletes): **0.7% bodyweight/week** kept lean mass and improved strength and power better than 1.4%/week [53].
  - 97 → 94 kg over about 15 wk is **0.2 kg/wk, about 0.2% bodyweight/wk** (calc). That is well under 0.5–1%, so the performance cost should be minimal.
  - Suggested pattern: about 0.25–0.35 kg/wk in weeks 1–10, then near-maintenance in the final 4–5 weeks (race-specific quality and taper). **No deficit in race week.**
- **Energy per kg lost:**
  - The "7700 kcal/kg" rule (3500 kcal/lb) overestimates the deficit needed in **lean people**, who lose a higher proportion of lean tissue [63].
  - For 3–4 kg over about 105 days, plan an average deficit of **about 250–400 kcal/day** (calc).
  - Protect lean mass with protein and 2×/wk resistance training [53,57].

### E2. Estimated daily energy for this athlete

- **The InBody "BMR" is an equation, not a measurement.** InBody estimates BMR from fat-free mass (about 370 + 21.6 × FFM; heuristic formula, not verified for his device).
  - At 94 kg and about 12% fat, FFM ≈ 82.7 kg, giving ≈ 2155 kcal. This matches his reported 2150 (calc).
  - At 97 kg and about 13% fat, FFM ≈ 84.4 kg, giving ≈ **2190 kcal** (calc).
- **Total daily energy (estimates):**

| Day type | Non-exercise energy (BMR × about 1.35–1.45) | Exercise energy | Maintenance | **Target intake (about 300 kcal deficit, averaged)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rest or easy day (≤45 min easy) | 2950–3150 | 0–400 | 3000–3500 | **≈2800–3100** |
| Hard day (HIIT + strength, or a HYROX simulation, 75–120 min) | 2950–3150 | 700–1100 | 3700–4200 | **≈3400–3800** |
| **Weekly average** (about 4 hard / 3 easy days) | | | about 3500–3800 | **about 3200–3450** |

- Exercise energy example: 60 min on the bike at an average of about 200 W is about 780 kcal, assuming 22% efficiency (calc).
- **Uncertainty is about ±10–15%.** Calibrate with **7-day average morning weight** every 2 weeks. If the loss is under 0.15 kg/wk, cut about 150 kcal/day. If over 0.4 kg/wk, add about 150–200 kcal/day (heuristic).

### E3. Macronutrients

- **Protein:**
  - 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercisers. **2.3–3.1 g/kg/day** may maximise lean mass retention during a deficit in resistance-trained people [57].
  - Gains in fat-free mass plateau at about **1.6 g/kg/day** (break point 1.62) [58].
  - **Target: 1.8–2.2 g/kg, about 175–210 g/day**, spread over 4–5 feedings of about 0.25–0.4 g/kg (about 25–40 g) [57].
- **Carbohydrate, "fuel for the work required"** [59,60]:
  - **Hard days** (HIIT, simulations, ≥75 min of training): **5–7 g/kg, about 480–650 g**. Concentrate it before, during and after sessions.
  - **Easy or rest days:** **3–5 g/kg, about 290–480 g**.
  - Do **not** do HIIT in a low-carbohydrate state. Low-glycogen ("train-low") work increases cell signalling but does not reliably improve performance [60].
- **Fat:** about 0.8–1.0 g/kg (about 75–95 g); the rest of the energy (heuristic).
- **Example hard day (calc):** 200 g protein + 550 g carbohydrate + 85 g fat ≈ 3765 kcal.
- **Example easy day (calc):** 200 g protein + 330 g carbohydrate + 80 g fat ≈ 2840 kcal.

### E4. Energy availability and RED-S

- **Definition:** EA = (energy intake − exercise energy) / fat-free mass [54].
- **Threshold:**
  - The classic **30 kcal/kg FFM/day** threshold comes from **women** (LH pulsatility; Loucks & Thuma 2003) [55].
  - In men, 4 days at **15 kcal/kg FFM** cut leptin by about 55% and insulin by about 35%. Testosterone and T3 did not change significantly [56].
  - The 2023 IOC consensus treats low EA as a spectrum from adaptable to problematic, not a single cutoff [54].
- **His numbers:** at maintenance his EA is only about 33–37 kcal/kg FFM, because his FFM is large (calc).
  - A hard day at 3700 kcal intake with 900 kcal exercise gives EA ≈ 33. A rest day at 2850 kcal gives ≈ 34 (calc).
  - **App rule: keep estimated daily EA at or above about 25–30 kcal/kg FFM, and never below 25 on consecutive hard days** (heuristic).
- **RED-S warning signs** [54]: persistent fatigue, low libido or loss of morning erections, recurrent illness, mood changes, stalled performance, **recurrent bone stress injuries**. Any of these should trigger a diet break and a medical review.

### E5. Hydration and sodium (indoor HYROX)

- **Daily or training:** prevent bodyweight loss above 2% from water deficit [61].
- **Measure sweat rate** in a simulation: (weight before − weight after + fluid drunk) ÷ hours.
- **Pre-race:** about **5–7 mL/kg (about 500–650 mL)** at least 4 h before, plus a salty meal [61].
- **Electrolyte drinks** with sodium and carbohydrate can beat water in some circumstances [61]. A typical target is about 20–30 mmol/L sodium (about 460–700 mg/L), as stated in ACSM 2007 (not re-verified from full text).
- **During the race (about 85–100 min, warm indoor hall):** sip about 150–250 mL every 20–30 min at aid points (heuristic), about 0.4–0.8 L in total depending on sweat rate. For a heavy or salty sweater, add about 300–600 mg sodium (drink or capsule) (heuristic).
- **After:** about 1.25–1.5 L per kg of body mass lost, with sodium (a commonly cited rule; not verified from the full text of [59] or [61]).

### E6. Race-day fueling

- **Evening before:** carbohydrate-rich dinner (about 6–8 g/kg for the day) (heuristic within the ranges in [59]).
- **2–4 h before:** **1–4 g/kg carbohydrate** (for example about 2 g/kg ≈ 190 g: rice or oats, banana, honey, low fibre and low fat) [59].
- **Caffeine:** **3 mg/kg (about 290 mg)** about 60 min before, if habituated and tested in training. The effective range is 3–6 mg/kg (minimum maybe 2); doses around 9 mg/kg bring side effects [62].
- **During (about 90 min):** **30–60 g/h** carbohydrate is recommended for 1–2.5 h efforts [59]. In practice, 1 gel (about 25–40 g) at about 35–45 min, optionally a second at about 65–75 min, with water. Rehearse this in simulations.
- **Weight on race day:** do not cut weight in the final week. A dehydrated, glycogen-depleted 94 kg is slower than a fuelled 94.5 kg (heuristic).

## F. Sleep, HRV, readiness metrics and load progression

### F1. Sleep

- The 2021 expert consensus (Walsh et al., BJSM): athletes are often short sleepers (under 7 h) with fragmented sleep. It recommends **individualised sleep goals** over rigid targets. Evidence on napping and sleep extension is still limited [73].
- **Practical:** 7.5–9 h in bed, consistent timing, caffeine cut-off about 8 h before bed (heuristic).
- **App rule:** fewer than 6 h of sleep on 2 nights in a row → downgrade the next HIIT to easy (heuristic).

### F2. HRV-guided training

| Study | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Kiviniemi 2007 [64] | 26 moderately fit men; HRV group did high intensity only when HRV was stable or rising | VO2peak change did not differ between groups. Authors concluded HRV can be used effectively to prescribe daily training. (Max running velocity results not re-verified.) [MODERATE] |
| Vesterinen 2016 [65] | Recreational runners | HRV can individualise the timing of vigorous sessions [MODERATE] |
| Javaloyes 2019 [66], 2020 [67] | Well-trained cyclists | HRV-guided training improved peak power, power at the second ventilatory threshold and 40-min time trial; between-group differences were small or non-significant [MODERATE] |
| Granero-Gallegos 2020 meta-analysis [68] | Endurance athletes | Small positive effect on VO2max (ES 0.40) [MODERATE] |
| 2021 meta-analyses [69,70] | — | Medium effect on submaximal markers; small, **non-significant** effect on VO2peak and performance; **fewer non-responders** [MODERATE] |

- **Conclusion:** HRV guidance probably does not raise average gains much, but it may reduce non-response and maladaptation. Useful as a **modifier**, not the main driver.
- **Protocol** [71,72]:
  - Measure **lnRMSSD each morning** (1–5 min, same position, after waking and using the toilet).
  - Use the **7-day rolling average** compared with your **60-day mean ± 0.5 SD**, a commonly used smallest worthwhile change (exact SD multiplier not verified).
  - At least 3–5 valid readings per week [71].
- **App rule** (heuristic, modelled on [64,66]):
  - 7-day average within the normal band → do the planned hard session.
  - Below the band → swap hard for easy or rest, and combine with the MTSS traffic light.
  - Above the band for several days with fatigue → possible parasympathetic saturation; consider an easy day.

### F3. Garmin Training Readiness and resting heart rate

- **Garmin Training Readiness** is a proprietary composite (sleep, recovery time, HRV status, load). **I found no peer-reviewed validation.** Use it as a secondary signal only. [Evidence gap]
- **Resting and submaximal heart rate:**
  - Measures from 5-min resting recordings and 30–60 s of submaximal exercise heart rate are the most useful heart-rate monitoring tools.
  - Interpret changes against measurement error and the smallest important change, plus the training context [72].
  - **Heuristic flag:** morning resting HR more than 5–7 bpm above the 7-day baseline, together with a low HRV trend or poor sleep → easy day.
  - A falling submaximal HR at a fixed bike power (for example HR at 180 W over 5 min) is a good weekly fitness marker [72].

### F4. ACWR and the "10% rule"

- **ACWR (acute-to-chronic workload ratio):** has substantial statistical and conceptual problems (mathematical coupling, artefacts, unsupported causal assumptions) [74]. **Do not build app logic on ACWR "sweet spots."** In Frandsen 2025, weekly ACWR actually showed a protective, negative pattern [77].
- **"10% rule":** an RCT in 532 novices found that a 13-week 10%-rule programme did **not** reduce injuries (20.8% vs 20.3%) [75].
- **Weekly increases over 30%:** associated with more distance-related injuries in novices [76].
- **Session spikes (best current evidence):** a single run more than 10% longer than the longest run in the past 30 days increased overuse injury rate (hazard ratio 1.64–2.28) [77]. [MODERATE; observational, self-reported]
- **App load rules:**
  1. No single run longer than **110% of the longest run in the past 30 days** [77].
  2. Weekly running volume increase ≤10–20% while returning from MTSS (conservative heuristic); hard cap 30% [76].
  3. Keep a **recovery week every 3–4 weeks**, with total volume about 30–40% lower (heuristic).
  4. Track bike and rower minutes separately from running. Bike and rower load **does not count toward tibial load**.

## G. Sample 16-week framework (application, expert opinion)

**Typical week in weeks 5–12** (adjust using the traffic light, HRV and sleep):
- **Mon:** Bike 4×4 or 4×8 [1,9] + lower-body strength (soleus, calf, posterior chain).
- **Tue:** Easy run (R-stage dose) + easy row 30–40 min.
- **Wed:** Rower or SkiErg 30/15, 2–3 sets × 13 [2,3] + upper-body and station technique (wall balls, sled).
- **Thu:** Easy bike 45–75 min at ≤150 bpm.
- **Fri:** Run intervals (stage-appropriate) or rest.
- **Sat:** HYROX-specific compromised session (for example 4–8 × [1 km run or 1 km bike/row + 1 station]) at threshold to race effort, + strength 2.
- **Sun:** Off, or easy bike or row 60–90 min.

**Blocks:**
- **Weeks 1–4:** base and return to run; 2 HIIT/wk on bike and rower.
- **Weeks 5–8:** build to 2–3 HIIT/wk; first retest at week 8.
- **Weeks 9–12:** add race simulations; reduce the deficit.
- **Weeks 13–15:** race-specific work at maintenance calories; retest week 15.
- **Week 16:** taper (volume −40–50%, keep intensity).

## Key evidence gaps

- No trials on **rowing or SkiErg transfer** to running performance.
- No trials on the **minimum running dose** needed to keep running economy or tissue tolerance.
- No validated **seated soleus strength norms**.
- No validated **pain thresholds for bone-related MTSS** (the pain-monitoring model comes from tendinopathy).
- No peer-reviewed validation of **Garmin Training Readiness**.
- HYROX physiology rests on **a single small study** [15].
- MTSS treatment evidence is low quality overall [32].
